  • Recognize subtle conversational tactics used to bypass standard identity checks and organizational ticketing workflows.
  • Establish and execute strict verification protocols for unannounced administrative or directory data requests.

1. Defining Pretexting

Pretexting is a form of social engineering where an attacker invents an elaborate scenario (a "pretext") and assumes a fabricated persona to trick a target into surrendering confidential information or granting unauthorized access.

Unlike impulsive phishing emails designed to provoke immediate panic, pretexting often involves a slow-burn, trust-building strategy. The adversary engages the remote worker across multiple casual interactions, laying a foundation of rapport and credibility before making a request.

2. Pretexting Persona Construction

Threat actors research organizational roles to design believable characters:

  • The External Auditor / Compliance Officer: The attacker claims to be conducting an urgent "data privacy audit" or "software licensing check." They request internal directory charts, system architecture documents, or employee listings.
  • The New Vendor / Subcontractor: The actor poses as a newly hired third-party service provider who needs "sample data files" or access to internal staging servers to prepare for an upcoming project.
  • The Distressed Remote Colleague: The scammer impersonates a fellow remote employee locked out of their system while travelling, appealing to empathy to convince the target to share internal files or forward login details.

3. Bypassing Workflow Protocols

Pretexters rely on social norms—such as politeness, willingness to help, and deference to authority—to convince targets to ignore standard operational procedures. Common tactics include:

  • Resisting Official Ticketing: The pretexter actively discourages the victim from opening a formal IT ticket or routing the request through administrative review (e.g., "Opening a ticket takes 3 days, and the VP needs this file in 10 minutes").
  • Flattery and Sympathy: Manipulating the remote worker's emotions to make them feel like a helpful savior.

4. Real-World Remote Work Scenario

Scenario: The Fake HR Benefits Survey

David, a remote administrative assistant, receives a connection request and direct message on LinkedIn from "Elena, Senior HR Director at Global HR Solutions." Elena sends a friendly message: "Hi David, your company has contracted us to review remote employee benefit satisfaction. We are conducting brief 5-minute interviews."

Over three days of polite messaging, Elena builds rapport. On day four, she sends an "Employee Verification Portal" link, asking David to confirm his corporate ID, home address, and internal manager's email. Because the interaction felt natural over multiple days, David submits the data without checking with his actual internal HR department.

5. Verification & Structural Controls

  1. Never Bypass Formal Ticketing: Require all administrative, data, or access requests to go through official, authenticated ticketing systems.
  2. Verify Third-Party Credentials Independently: If an external auditor or vendor contacts you, confirm their contract status with your manager or procurement lead before sharing any files.
  3. Be Wary of Overly Elaborate Explanations: Legitimate corporate interactions rarely require complicated stories to justify bypassing standard security checks.

Red Flag Indicators & Defensive Checklist

  • Unsolicited Multi-Day Outreach: Contacts who build casual rapport over several days before making data or file requests.
  • Pressure to Avoid Official Channels: Requests to avoid logging tickets, emailing official distribution lists, or involving managers.
  • Inquiries Into Internal Operations: Casual questions about organizational hierarchies, software versions, or internal tools.
  • Appeals to Empathy / Authority: Stories emphasizing extreme distress, impending disciplinary action, or executive deadlines to force compliance.
